# Never Split the Difference — PM Diagnostic Quiz
## Three Question Banks: Framework, Scoring, and Answer Guide

---

## Quiz Architecture

**Three clusters, 12 frameworks, 13 questions per bank.**

| Cluster | Frameworks | Questions per Bank |
|---------|-----------|-------------------|
| Cluster 1: Listening & Empathy | Tactical Empathy, Mirroring, Labeling | 4 |
| Cluster 2: Strategic Influence | Accusation Audit, Calibrated Questions, No-Oriented Questions, Summary/Paraphrasing, "That's Right" vs "You're Right," Three Negotiator Types | 6 |
| Cluster 3: Execution & Agreement | Loss Aversion/Bending Reality, Black Swans, Implementation "How" Questions | 3 |

**Scoring:** Each cluster scored 0–100 based on questions answered correctly. Overall score is weighted average (Cluster 1: 30%, Cluster 2: 50%, Cluster 3: 20% — weighting reflects PM relevance). Estimated completion time: 7–10 minutes.

**Design principle:** Every question has one clearly correct answer and at least one "trap" answer — a response that feels intuitively right but represents a Voss mistake. The trap answers are what make this diagnostic, not just a knowledge test.

### CLUSTER 1: Listening & Empathy

---

**Q1 | Framework: Tactical Empathy**

You're presenting a roadmap to your engineering lead. She's been quiet throughout, arms crossed, giving short one-word answers. The meeting is halfway through. What's your first move?

- A) Explain the business rationale in more detail — she may not have enough context to evaluate the direction
- B) Ask her directly: "Do you have concerns about this roadmap?"
- C) Acknowledge what you observe: "It seems like there's something about this direction that doesn't sit right with you"
- D) Keep the meeting moving and address concerns in a follow-up 1:1

**✓ Correct: C**
Tactical empathy means naming what you observe without demanding confirmation or denial. "It seems like" is non-accusatory and creates space without confrontation. If you're right, she'll open up. If you're slightly off, she'll correct you — and either way, you get real information.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Logic-first response that completely bypasses her emotional state. Explaining more context to someone who's emotionally disengaged makes them more defensive, not less.
- B: A direct yes/no question that feels respectful but is actually confrontational — it forces her to either publicly commit to having a problem or retreat to "I'm fine."
- D: Avoids the tension entirely. The concern will resurface at a worse moment.

---

**Q2 | Framework: Mirroring**

Your engineering manager says in a planning meeting: "This timeline is completely unrealistic and it's going to burn the team out." You want him to keep talking so you understand the real concern. What's your best response?

- A) "I hear you — let's look at what we can cut from scope to make this more realistic."
- B) "Burn the team out?"
- C) "What specifically about the timeline concerns you most?"
- D) "I understand. What timeline would actually work for the team?"

**✓ Correct: B**
Mirroring repeats the last 1–3 critical words with a slight upward inflection. The manager will now elaborate on what "burn the team out" actually means to him — past trauma, specific workload concerns, a perception that he's never been heard on this before. You learn far more than any question would surface.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Jumps immediately to problem-solving before you've understood the real concern. You might cut the wrong scope.
- C: A calibrated question is a good technique but the wrong sequence — you haven't let him elaborate yet. Use the mirror first, then a question if needed.
- D: Also rushes to solution. "What timeline would work?" assumes the problem is timeline, not something deeper.

---

**Q3 | Framework: Labeling**

A senior stakeholder says "I'm fine with whatever you decide" in a flat, slightly clipped tone. His words say yes but his energy says no. What do you do?

- A) Take the agreement at face value — he's a senior person who says what he means
- B) Thank him for his flexibility and proceed
- C) Say: "It seems like you might not be fully on board with this direction"
- D) Ask: "Are you sure you're okay with this?"

**✓ Correct: C**
Labeling the incongruence between his words and tone surfaces the real feeling. If you're wrong, he'll correct you — "No, I genuinely mean it." If you're right, he'll open up — "Honestly, I do have a concern." Either way, you prevent counterfeit agreement from becoming an implementation problem later.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A and B: Accept "you're right" disguised as "I'm fine." Surface-level agreement that evaporates at the implementation stage.
- D: A yes/no question makes it easy to retreat right back to "Yes, I'm sure." Doesn't create space for genuine disclosure.

---

**Q4 | Framework: Mirroring**

In a prioritization meeting, a product stakeholder says: "We keep saying customer retention is the priority but our roadmap doesn't reflect that." You want more information before you respond. What do you say?

- A) Defend the roadmap by walking through the tradeoffs that led to it
- B) "Our roadmap doesn't reflect that?"
- C) "That's a fair point — let's revisit the prioritization framework."
- D) "What would a retention-focused roadmap actually look like to you?"

**✓ Correct: B**
Mirror to extract what they mean before you respond. "Doesn't reflect that" could mean many things — specific features are missing, proportions are wrong, retention isn't in the OKRs, or they have a specific initiative in mind. You can't know until they elaborate.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Defensive. You're arguing before you understand the accusation.
- C: Concedes ground without understanding what ground is actually being contested.
- D: Good instinct — but premature. You're jumping to solution design before fully understanding the grievance. Mirror first, then ask.

### CLUSTER 2: Strategic Influence

**Q5 | Framework: Accusation Audit**

You need to tell your CPO that the feature she has publicly championed for two quarters needs to be cut from the roadmap. How do you open the conversation?

- A) "I have some difficult news about the feature — can we walk through the data together?"
- B) "I know this is going to feel like we're not valuing your judgment, and that the timing is terrible given how hard you've pushed for this."
- C) "Based on our capacity analysis, we need to make some hard prioritization calls this quarter."
- D) "I want to be transparent — I've been wrestling with how to bring this to you."

**✓ Correct: B**
The accusation audit names the worst things she might think or feel before she thinks them. She was probably already imagining: "This is a judgment on me," and "This is terrible timing." You voiced it first — now those accusations sound exaggerated when held up to the light, and her defenses lower before the news even lands.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Data-forward. Logically respectful but emotionally tone-deaf for this moment. She'll experience it as minimizing.
- C: Business-speak that depersonalizes — feels dismissive of her investment.
- D: Shows personal vulnerability but doesn't actually address what she's likely fearing.

---

**Q6 | Framework: Calibrated Questions**

Your engineering lead keeps saying a core architecture component needs "more time" but won't give specifics. You need clarity. What do you ask?

- A) "Can you give me a timeline for when it'll be ready?"
- B) "Why does this keep needing more time?"
- C) "What specifically would need to be true for you to feel confident in the timeline?"
- D) "Are we talking days or weeks?"

**✓ Correct: C**
An open-ended "what" question that invites specificity without triggering defensiveness. Forces him to articulate the actual blockers — technical dependencies, team capacity, unresolved design questions — which gives you something to work with.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Closed question ("can you") that produces a number without the reasoning behind it.
- B: "Why" is accusatory in Voss's framework. It sounds like you're challenging his competence rather than trying to understand.
- D: Binary — caps the answer before it starts and produces no useful information about the actual blocker.

**Q7 | Framework: No-Oriented Questions**

You've sent a proposal to a cross-functional partner three times with no response. What do you send next?

- A) "Following up again — can you let me know your thoughts by end of week?"
- B) "Have you had a chance to review the proposal I sent?"
- C) "Have you given up on this initiative?"
- D) "I wanted to check in — is this still a priority for your team?"

**✓ Correct: C**
No-oriented question. The phrase "given up" triggers an immediate "no, of course not!" response that breaks the silence and re-engages. It creates urgency without pleading and doesn't give them an easy way to continue ignoring you.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A, B, D: All require positive engagement to answer — they need to choose to respond, which is exactly what they've been avoiding. You're asking them to do the same thing that hasn't worked three times.

**Q8 | Framework: Three Negotiator Types**

Your engineering manager responds to messages within minutes, visibly loses patience in long meetings, always wants the bottom line first, and gets frustrated when discussions circle back to previously covered ground. What type is he and how do you adapt?

- A) Analyst — send detailed documentation in advance so he can process before the meeting
- B) Accommodator — build relationship time into meetings, open with personal connection
- C) Assertive — lead with your conclusion, keep communications brief, use mirrors to show he's been heard
- D) Assertive — match his directness and forceful energy to establish mutual respect

**✓ Correct: C**
Classic Assertive profile. Assertives want to be heard first — they literally cannot listen until they feel understood. Mirror them, state your conclusion first, and keep it tight. Mirroring is particularly effective because it signals that you've absorbed their position, which unlocks their ability to hear yours.

**Why the traps fail:**
- D: The most tempting wrong answer. Matching assertive energy feels natural but creates a power contest. Assertives want to be heard, not challenged.
- A: Wrong type identification. Analysts are deliberate and process-oriented; this person is reactive and time-focused.
- B: Completely wrong type.

**Q9 | Framework: Summary / Paraphrasing**

After a long discussion where a stakeholder explains why a feature keeps getting deprioritized, you want to check your understanding before proposing a solution. What's your move?

- A) "So it sounds like you feel this feature has been overlooked, your team's credibility is on the line, and there's real concern that leadership doesn't see its strategic value — is that right?"
- B) "I hear you. Let me share what we can do to address this."
- C) "You're absolutely right that this has been a problem. Here's what I'd propose."
- D) "Let me make sure I understand — you want this feature prioritized?"

**✓ Correct: A**
A complete summary combining paraphrase plus emotional acknowledgment, designed to trigger "That's right." The goal is to demonstrate you understood both the facts and the feelings — overlooked feature, credibility stakes, leadership visibility. Only after "that's right" do you earn the right to pitch.

**Why the traps fail:**
- B, C: Skip to solution before earning trust. The stakeholder hasn't confirmed you understand them — you've just told them you do.
- D: Too reductive. A one-sentence summary fails to demonstrate the depth of understanding needed for genuine alignment. More likely to produce "well, sort of..." than "that's right."

**Q10 | Framework: "That's Right" vs "You're Right"**

In a tense roadmap review, a stakeholder says: "I get it, you're right, we can't do everything." How do you interpret this?

- A) Genuine alignment — they understand the constraints and are on board with the decision
- B) Polite capitulation — they want the conversation to end but haven't genuinely bought in
- C) Partial alignment — logical understanding without emotional buy-in
- D) A signal to move quickly before they change their mind

**✓ Correct: B**
"You're right" is Voss's clearest signal of disengagement. It means "please stop talking." It is not the same as "That's right" — which reflects genuine understanding. When someone says "you're right," they've stopped processing and started managing the conversation to an end.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A, D: Treating "you're right" as real agreement is the most common and costly mistake. This stakeholder will either re-raise the objection later or passively fail to support the decision.
- C: Partially right about the logical/emotional split but misses the key Voss insight that "you're right" specifically signals conversational withdrawal.

### CLUSTER 3: Execution & Agreement

---

**Q11 | Framework: Loss Aversion / Bending Reality**

You're trying to get VP buy-in for a new analytics investment. Which framing is most persuasive?

- A) "This investment will improve our decision-making speed by 30%."
- B) "Teams with this capability are shipping features 30% faster — every quarter we wait, we fall further behind."
- C) "This is a $200K investment with a projected 3x ROI over 18 months."
- D) "Industry leaders in our space have already made this investment."

**✓ Correct: B**
Loss framing activates loss aversion — research shows losses are felt roughly twice as strongly as equivalent gains. The answer combines competitor threat (they're pulling ahead), ongoing cost of waiting (every quarter), and implicit blame assignment (falling behind is a choice we're making). This is far more motivating than the equivalent gain framing.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Pure gain framing — positive but weak. "30% faster" feels abstract and optional.
- C: ROI is gain framing in disguise. VPs have seen enough ROI projections to discount them heavily.
- D: Social proof — valid supporting evidence but lacks the loss urgency needed to force a decision.

---

**Q12 | Framework: Black Swans**

You've been negotiating with a stakeholder for three weeks. They agree to everything in meetings but nothing moves. What's most likely happening, and what do you do?

- A) They're too busy to prioritize execution — offer to handle the implementation yourself
- B) There's a hidden constraint — authority, budget, or approval they don't have and can't reveal. Ask: "How does moving forward affect your team's priorities?"
- C) They're conflict-avoidant and giving counterfeit agreement — use the Rule of Three to verify commitment
- D) The initiative isn't actually a priority — send a "have you given up?" message to force a decision

**✓ Correct: B**
The Black Swan here is a hidden constraint. "Yes" without movement is the clearest signal that your counterpart is constrained — they lack the authority, budget, or upstream approval to deliver what they're agreeing to. A calibrated "how" question gets them to reveal the constraint without putting them on the spot.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Takes ownership of their problem without understanding it. You might be doing work that can't be used.
- C: Conflict avoidance is plausible but doesn't explain a three-week pattern. Rule of Three tests sincerity, not capability.
- D: Gives them an easy exit before you understand what's actually blocking progress.

---

**Q13 | Framework: Implementation "How" Questions**

Your VP has just verbally approved your roadmap in a 1:1. Before the meeting ends, what do you ask?

- A) "Great — I'll send a summary and start the planning process."
- B) "How do we make sure engineering, design, and data are all genuinely aligned before we kick off?"
- C) "Who else should I loop in as we start execution?"
- D) "What timeline are you expecting for the first milestone?"

**✓ Correct: B**
Implementation "how" questions force your VP to think through cross-functional dependencies himself — creating shared ownership rather than leaving alignment entirely to you. This also surfaces any behind-the-table stakeholders or constraints before they derail execution.

**Why the traps fail:**
- C: Close, but a closed question. Produces a list, not ownership. The VP lists names; you do all the alignment work.
- A: Assumes the verbal yes is implementation-ready. It's often not.
- D: Timeline without alignment is the wrong sequence.

**Q1 | Framework: Tactical Empathy**

You're in sprint planning. A senior engineer says "I'll do it" to taking on a complex task, but his tone is flat and his posture is closed. What's the right move?

- A) Accept the commitment — he said yes and he's a professional
- B) Say: "It seems like this task might be creating some concerns you haven't mentioned"
- C) Ask: "Are you sure you can handle this on top of everything else?"
- D) Redistribute the task to someone who volunteers more willingly

**✓ Correct: B**
Tactical empathy names the underlying emotional state. A flat "I'll do it" from an experienced engineer often signals hidden concerns — an unrealistic estimate, dependency on another team, or a technical approach he disagrees with. Naming it creates the opening.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Accepts counterfeit commitment. You'll find out at the sprint review that the task is incomplete.
- C: A yes/no that makes it easy to retreat to "No, I'm fine." Also slightly condescending.
- D: Avoids the conversation you actually need to have.

**Q2 | Framework: Mirroring**

A product stakeholder says: "This is the third time we've had to change direction because of shifting priorities." You want to understand the frustration before responding. What do you say?

- A) "I completely understand your frustration. Let me explain the context behind the changes."
- B) "Shifting priorities?"
- C) "You're right, this has been inconsistent. How can we make the next change easier?"
- D) "I hear you — what would a more stable process look like?"

**✓ Correct: B**
Mirror the most emotionally loaded phrase. "Shifting priorities" is the frustration anchor. They'll elaborate on what that has meant for them — wasted work, lost credibility, resource confusion — information you can't get by explaining or problem-solving.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Explaining context is the most counterproductive response. You're defending the behavior before understanding their experience of it.
- C: Concedes without understanding what "inconsistent" means to them specifically.
- D: Jumps to solution before the concern has been fully heard.

**Q3 | Framework: Labeling**

You're presenting a new workflow framework to your design team. Halfway through, the lead designer interrupts: "We tried something similar before." What do you do?

- A) Ask: "What happened when you tried it before?"
- B) Say: "It seems like there's a concern that this might lead to the same outcome as last time."
- C) Acknowledge and pivot: "That's useful context — this approach is actually different because..."
- D) Say: "It seems like you're skeptical of new frameworks in general."

**✓ Correct: B**
Labels the underlying fear — repeating past failure — not just the surface statement. This is more likely to get her to open up than a question, because she feels understood before she has to explain herself.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Calibrated question is the right tool eventually, but label first. The question comes after the emotion is acknowledged.
- C: Pivots to selling before understanding. She hasn't said why the previous attempt failed.
- D: The label is too broad and slightly accusatory — it misidentifies the concern.

**Q4 | Framework: Tactical Empathy**

You just told an engineering manager that a feature his team spent six weeks building will not ship due to a strategy pivot. He goes completely silent. What do you do first?

- A) Fill the silence by explaining the business reasoning — he deserves to understand why
- B) Say: "I imagine this feels like six weeks of your team's work being wasted."
- C) Ask: "What are you thinking right now?"
- D) Give him space — suggest a follow-up conversation once he's had time to process

**✓ Correct: B**
This is tactical empathy applied to bad news delivery — name the likely emotional experience before asking for any cognitive processing. "Wasted work" is the fear. Naming it shows you understand what this actually means to him and his team.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Logic in response to emotional shock is almost always counterproductive. He can't hear the reasoning yet.
- C: "What are you thinking?" when someone is in shock is an unfair question. They may not know yet.
- D: Delays the empathy he needs right now. Space without acknowledgment reads as indifference.

**Q5 | Framework: Accusation Audit**

You need to ask a cross-functional team that's already stretched thin to take on additional work this quarter. How do you open the request?

- A) "I know you're all slammed, but I wouldn't be asking if it wasn't truly critical."
- B) "I want to be upfront — I know this feels like we're piling onto an already impossible list, and you're probably thinking your team always gets handed the extra work no one else will take."
- C) "This is a high-priority request from the CPO, so I want to make sure we're aligned on urgency."
- D) "I have a request, and before I share it, I want to hear your constraints."

**✓ Correct: B**
Full accusation audit. Names both the factual concern (overloaded) and the deeper resentment (always the team that gets the overflow). Hearing their unspoken frustrations voiced out loud deflates them — the team often responds by arguing against their own worst interpretation.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: "But" negates everything before it. "I know you're slammed but..." signals that you know and don't care enough to change the ask.
- C: Authority-based framing generates resentment, not engagement.
- D: Responsive but reactive — waits for objections instead of defusing them proactively.

**Q6 | Framework: Calibrated Questions**

Your CEO asks why a major feature is delayed. You need to understand what specifically concerns him before you respond. What do you ask?

- A) "Is your concern primarily about the timeline or the scope?"
- B) "What aspect of this delay is most important for us to address right now?"
- C) "Why do you think this happened?"
- D) "Can you tell me more about what's worrying you?"

**✓ Correct: B**
An open "what" question that gives him control of where to take the answer — he might be concerned about a customer commitment, a board presentation, a competitor launch, or team morale. You can't solve the right problem until you know which problem it is.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Binary — limits his answer to two options before you know which one matters.
- C: "Why do you think this happened?" sounds like you're asking the CEO to diagnose your team's failure. Tone-deaf in this direction.
- D: Vague and open-ended in an unproductive way — invites a long response without direction.

**Q7 | Framework: No-Oriented Questions**

You want to propose a new data instrumentation approach to a VP who has historically been skeptical of analytics investments. How do you open?

- A) "I have a new analytics proposal I think addresses your previous concerns."
- B) "Would it be completely off base to take 20 minutes to walk through a new approach to how we're instrumenting the product?"
- C) "I know you've heard a lot of analytics pitches. Would you be open to hearing one more?"
- D) "I wanted to get your input on an instrumentation initiative we're evaluating."

**✓ Correct: B**
No-oriented framing. "Would it be completely off base..." invites a "no, that wouldn't be off base" response while giving her a genuine exit. Low-threat, low-pressure framing that doesn't trigger the "here we go again" defense.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Presumes your proposal solved her past concerns — presumptuous without knowing what they were.
- C: Condescending — acknowledges her fatigue then asks her to endure more anyway.
- D: Procedurally fine but misses the opportunity to defuse the historical skepticism with no-framing.

**Q8 | Framework: Three Negotiator Types**

Your designer frequently says "whatever you think is best" in meetings, agrees to everything in the room, but then raises concerns via Slack hours later or in side conversations. What's happening and what's the risk?

- A) She's an Analyst — she needs more processing time, give her 24 hours before expecting a decision
- B) She's an Accommodator giving counterfeit agreement — her real concerns are going underground and will resurface as passive resistance
- C) She's an Assertive who tests ideas by first agreeing then stress-testing privately
- D) She's an Accommodator who genuinely defers to expertise — this is a healthy dynamic

**✓ Correct: B**
Classic Accommodator profile. Accommodators hate conflict so much that they produce "yes" in the room and then route real concerns through low-confrontation channels. The risk is that you build with false alignment — her concerns re-emerge at sprint review, launch, or retrospective.

**Why the traps fail:**
- D: The most dangerous trap — taking the deference at face value produces the exact dynamic that leads to late-stage surprises.
- A: Analysts are deliberate, not conflict-avoidant. They express disagreement; they just do it slowly and with data.
- C: Completely inverts the Assertive profile.

**Q9 | Framework: Summary / Paraphrasing**

After 40 minutes listening to an engineering director's concerns about a Q3 plan, you want to verify understanding before pitching anything. Which response is most likely to produce "That's right"?

- A) "So you're saying the timeline is too aggressive?"
- B) "It sounds like you're worried about the timeline, frustrated that the team wasn't consulted earlier, and concerned that technical debt isn't being accounted for — and underneath all of that is a real fear that if this fails, your team takes the blame publicly."
- C) "I understand your concerns. Let me address them one by one."
- D) "You're right that we have a lot to work through."

**✓ Correct: B**
A complete summary hitting factual concerns (timeline, consultation, tech debt) and the underlying emotional stakes (public blame). The deeper you go — especially naming the fear they haven't said out loud — the more likely "That's right."

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Too thin. One concern summarized out of several heard.
- C: Pivots to solution before earning trust — he hasn't confirmed you understood him.
- D: "You're right" from you signals you want the conversation to move on, not that you truly understood him.

**Q10 | Framework: "That's Right" vs "You're Right"**

You've just made a comprehensive case to your CPO for why a feature should be cut. She responds: "You've made some good points. I suppose you're right." What do you do?

- A) Thank her and move to next steps — you've earned the decision
- B) Pause and label: "It seems like there's still something giving you pause about this."
- C) Confirm: "So we're aligned on cutting it?"
- D) Send a follow-up email summarizing the decision while the conversation is fresh

**✓ Correct: B**
"You're right" is the disengagement signal. She's closing the conversation, not agreeing with the decision. Label the residual hesitation before moving forward — otherwise she reverses course later or quietly fails to support it.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A, C, D: All treat "I suppose you're right" as genuine commitment and move to execution. This is the exact path to the reversal that happens two weeks later in a different meeting.

**Q11 | Framework: Loss Aversion / Bending Reality**

Your team is debating whether to invest engineering capacity in reducing technical debt. Which argument is most persuasive to a risk-averse VP of Engineering?

- A) "Addressing this now will make the team 25% more productive over the next year."
- B) "Every quarter we delay, we're compounding the problem. At our current trajectory, this will cost twice the engineering time to fix in 12 months — and we've already seen it eat three sprints this quarter."
- C) "Industry best practice recommends allocating 20% of engineering capacity to debt reduction."
- D) "The team morale impact of working in poor code quality is real and measurable."

**✓ Correct: B**
Specific, recent loss framing (three wasted sprints this quarter) with an implied escalating future cost (double the effort in 12 months). Makes the status quo feel expensive and risky rather than safe and neutral.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Gain framing — sounds good but doesn't trigger the urgency that loss does.
- C: Social proof is supporting evidence, not a primary argument. A VP will say "we're not average."
- D: Real concern but soft and hard to quantify in the budget conversation.

**Q12 | Framework: Black Swans**

You've been trying to get sign-off on a vendor contract for six weeks. Legal keeps requesting minor revisions but never escalates real concerns and never closes. What Black Swan should you investigate, and how?

- A) Legal is understaffed — offer to expedite by handling more of the coordination yourself
- B) There's a hidden approval bottleneck — a senior stakeholder hasn't blessed this engagement and legal is stalling without being able to say so. Get face time and ask: "How does this contract affect your team's ability to close it?"
- C) The contract terms are genuinely problematic — request a full legal review call
- D) Legal isn't resourced for this type of contract — escalate to your legal team's manager

**✓ Correct: B**
"Minor revisions that never resolve" for six weeks is the Black Swan pattern of a hidden constraint. Someone who needs to approve this hasn't — legal is in an impossible position. A calibrated "how" question in a face-to-face moment is most likely to surface it.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Takes ownership of their process problem without understanding what's actually blocked.
- C: Genuine problems would have produced escalating, not trivial, revisions. The pattern doesn't fit.
- D: Escalation without information may accelerate or kill a deal you don't fully understand yet.

**Q13 | Framework: Implementation "How" Questions**

Your roadmap has just been approved in a VP review. Before the meeting ends, what's the most important question to ask?

- A) "Great — I'll put together a comms plan and project kickoff."
- B) "How do we make sure engineering, design, and data are genuinely aligned before we start?"
- C) "Who should I loop in during execution?"
- D) "What's the timeline expectation for the first milestone?"

**✓ Correct: B**
Forces the VP to own cross-functional alignment, not just approve the plan. She's now thinking through the dependencies herself — which surfaces any hidden blockers (misaligned engineering lead, budget gate, design resource constraint) before they hit you in week three.

**Why the traps fail:**
- C: Closed question that produces a list, not ownership or insight into hidden blockers.
- A: Assumes implementation will be smooth. Premature.
- D: Timeline before alignment is the wrong sequence. You can have a perfect timeline with a misaligned organization.

### CLUSTER 1: Listening & Empathy

**Q1 | Framework: Tactical Empathy**

You present a quarterly OKR update. The room goes quiet. Your VP of Engineering nods slowly but says nothing. A product stakeholder checks her phone. What should you do?

- A) Ask: "Does anyone have questions or concerns about the update?"
- B) Continue presenting — silence often means they're processing
- C) Say: "It seems like this update is raising concerns that haven't been voiced yet."
- D) Address it directly: "I know these numbers aren't where we hoped."

**✓ Correct: C**
Labels the group's emotional state without singling anyone out. Creates permission for honest reactions. "It seems like" is non-accusatory — it's an invitation, not a confrontation.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Yes/no question produces polite denial. Nobody wants to be the first to say the room is uncomfortable.
- B: Ignores clear signals. The discomfort will surface later in a less controllable setting.
- D: Explains the situation rather than inviting response. "I know the numbers aren't great" closes conversation rather than opening it.

**Q2 | Framework: Mirroring**

A stakeholder says: "I've been in five roadmap reviews this year and nothing ever actually changes." You want to draw out the frustration. Which phrase do you mirror?

- A) "Nothing ever actually changes?"
- B) "Five roadmap reviews?"
- C) "It sounds like you're frustrated with the process."
- D) "I understand. What would you want to see change?"

**✓ Correct: A**
Mirror the most emotionally loaded phrase — "nothing ever changes" carries the frustration and the implied futility. "Five roadmap reviews" is just a count. Mirroring the wrong phrase wastes the technique.

**Why the traps fail:**
- B: Mirrors the neutral detail, not the emotional core.
- C: This is labeling, not mirroring — a different technique. Good instinct, wrong tool for this moment.
- D: Jumps immediately to solution before the frustration has been fully heard.

**Q3 | Framework: Labeling**

Your lead engineer says "I just don't think this is technically feasible" about a feature leadership has already committed to. Best response?

- A) "It seems like there are some specific technical concerns about the current approach."
- B) "That's a serious concern — what specifically is the blocker?"
- C) "It seems like you're worried this will fail and your team will take the blame for something they didn't design."
- D) Say nothing — let him elaborate on his own.

**✓ Correct: C**
Goes beneath the surface concern (technical feasibility) to the underlying fear (being blamed for failure on something they didn't choose). The deeper label is more likely to unlock honest conversation — if correct, he'll open up about the real dynamic; if wrong, he'll correct you with useful information.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Valid label but shallow — stays on the surface technical concern. Misses the political/emotional dimension.
- B: Calibrated question, good technique but premature. Label the emotion first, then ask.
- D: Silence is fine but you've missed the opportunity to demonstrate you understand what this means to him.

**Q4 | Framework: Tactical Empathy**

You're inheriting a product from a PM who was deeply respected by the engineering team. Your first team meeting, you sense wariness and guardedness. How do you open?

- A) "I'm excited to be here and I want to start by hearing what's working and what isn't."
- B) "I know it's hard when a PM you respect moves on. You're probably wondering whether I'll understand what matters to this team the way she did."
- C) "Let me share a bit about my background and how I like to work."
- D) "I've been reviewing the roadmap and I have some initial thoughts I'd like to share."

**✓ Correct: B**
Proactively names the emotional state of the room — wariness about change, loyalty to the predecessor, uncertainty about you. This is an accusation audit applied to a first impression. It's the most disarming possible opening because you've said what they were all privately thinking.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Generic onboarding opener that ignores the emotional reality in the room.
- C: Self-focused at exactly the moment the team needs to feel acknowledged.
- D: The worst possible opening — immediately signals that you're in evaluation mode, which triggers defensiveness in a team that's already protective of their previous PM's work.

**Q5 | Framework: Accusation Audit**

You're about to propose cutting a feature that a key stakeholder has been publicly associated with for two quarters. How do you open?

- A) "I want to walk through some data before we make any decisions on this."
- B) "I know this is going to feel like a direct challenge to your judgment on something you've put yourself on the line for. You might be thinking this is political or that leadership has lost confidence in you. It isn't."
- C) "I want to be sensitive to the work you've put into this, and I think the data will help us think through it together."
- D) "This is a hard conversation, but we need to have an honest discussion about the future of this feature."

**✓ Correct: B**
Full accusation audit naming the specific fears: "judgment questioned," "political," "leadership lost confidence." These are the things she's been privately dreading. Hearing them spoken out loud — especially as something you're preemptively denying — defuses them before they can solidify into defensiveness.

**Why the traps fail:**
- C: Shows sensitivity but doesn't name the specific fears. Leaves them to fester.
- A: Data-led approach that bypasses the emotional reality entirely.
- D: Acknowledges difficulty without doing the specific work of defusing.

**Q6 | Framework: Calibrated Questions**

A senior stakeholder says: "I need this shipped in six weeks, no exceptions." You're not sure if the deadline is real or positional. What's your best response?

- A) "That's a very aggressive timeline. What can we cut to make it work?"
- B) "What would need to be true for six weeks to actually be achievable?"
- C) "Six weeks is very tight — can you help us understand the constraints driving this timeline?"
- D) "What happens if we don't hit six weeks?"

**✓ Correct: B**
Forces the stakeholder to think through the dependencies themselves — which reveals whether the deadline is tied to a real event (customer commitment, board meeting, competitive launch) or is positional pressure. If they can't answer it, the deadline may be negotiable.

**Why the traps fail:**
- D: Good question — understanding consequences of missing deadline is valuable — but B is better because it steers toward collaborative problem-solving rather than risk discussion.
- A: Assumes the deadline is fixed and immediately concedes on scope.
- C: "Can you help us understand..." is slightly passive. B is more direct and productive.

**Q7 | Framework: No-Oriented Questions**

A VP has said she'll review your proposal "soon" for three weeks running. What do you send?

- A) "Following up on the proposal — happy to answer any questions you have."
- B) "Is it safe to say this initiative isn't a priority right now?"
- C) "Have you given up on this initiative?"
- D) "Would it be wrong to assume we should pause this until next quarter?"

**✓ Correct: C**
The classic Voss re-engagement trigger. "Have you given up" produces an immediate "no, of course not" that breaks the silence and creates urgency. It forces her to actively re-engage rather than passively continue avoiding.

**Why the traps fail:**
- B, D: No-oriented but too soft — they explicitly give her permission to say "yes, let's pause it," which is the outcome you're trying to prevent.
- A: Standard follow-up that has already failed three times.

**Q8 | Framework: Three Negotiator Types**

Your data science lead sends 40-page analyses before every meeting, requests agendas 48 hours in advance, and goes noticeably silent when new information is introduced without warning. What type is this, and how do you adapt?

- A) Assertive — he needs to feel in control; let him set the agenda and own the analysis
- B) Analyst — he hates surprises; always send detailed pre-reads, never introduce new information cold, treat his silence as thinking rather than agreement or disagreement
- C) Accommodator — the lengthy analysis is an attempt to please; his silence is discomfort with conflict
- D) Analyst — agree with his analyses to build rapport and trust

**✓ Correct: B**
Classic Analyst. Methodical, thorough, time-independent, surprise-averse. The silence during new information is him processing — interrupting or pushing for a reaction is counterproductive. Pre-reads, advance notice, no cold surprises.

**Why the traps fail:**
- D: Agreeing with all his analyses without engaging signals you haven't read them — the worst signal to an Analyst who has invested heavily in rigor.
- A: Assertives are fast and bottom-line focused — the opposite of this profile.
- C: Accommodators avoid conflict through agreement — this person expresses himself through data, not silence.

**Q9 | Framework: Summary / Paraphrasing**

You've spent 40 minutes listening to a skeptical VP of Sales explain why the product doesn't meet his team's needs. You want to earn the right to present a solution. What do you say?

- A) "Thanks for sharing all of that. I think we have some options that address most of these concerns."
- B) "So if I'm hearing you right — the product is built for discovery, but your team lives in the close. The missing piece isn't features, it's workflow integration at the moment of truth. And every week without that is a quota miss your reps have to explain upward. Is that right?"
- C) "You've raised a lot of valid concerns. Let me take these back to the team and come back with a proposal."
- D) "I've been hearing similar feedback from others — let me share what we're planning to address it."

**✓ Correct: B**
A complete summary that hits the factual gap (discovery vs. close), the missing piece (workflow integration), and the emotional stakes (quota misses explained upward). When he says "That's right," you've earned the pitch and he's already partially sold.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Jumps straight to "we have options" before confirming understanding. He'll be skeptical before you start.
- C: Unnecessary deferral — you have enough to summarize now and lose momentum by leaving.
- D: Makes his specific concern generic. He'll feel unheard.

**Q10 | Framework: "That's Right" vs "You're Right"**

Midway through a contentious roadmap session, your lead engineer says: "Fine. You're right. Let's just do it your way." How do you interpret this and what do you do?

- A) This is hard-won agreement after working through his objections — proceed
- B) This is tactical withdrawal — he'll comply but won't be an engaged owner of the outcome. Label it: "It seems like you still have real concerns about this approach."
- C) This is passive aggression — "you're right" is his way of expressing frustration
- D) This is genuine buy-in — the "fine" signals that he worked through the resistance

**✓ Correct: B**
"You're right, let's do it your way" is disengagement, not alignment. He's handing you the decision while withdrawing ownership. The risk is compliance without investment — he'll execute minimally and the first problem will become "I told you so." Label it and surface the real concern.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A, D: "You're right" is not the same as "That's right." Treating it as genuine buy-in leads directly to the implementation failure you were trying to avoid.
- C: Reads it as emotional when it's actually strategic — Voss's insight is specifically about the language as a disengagement signal, not just frustration.

**Q11 | Framework: Loss Aversion / Bending Reality**

You're trying to get budget approval for UX research. Which argument is most effective with a VP who controls the budget?

- A) "Good UX research will help us build more user-centric features and improve satisfaction scores."
- B) "Companies that invest in UX research see 2x improvement in feature adoption."
- C) "Every feature we ship without research validation carries real rework risk. We've already seen this cost us three sprints this quarter — and without a change, the next three quarters look the same."
- D) "UX research is a standard investment at our stage of growth."

**✓ Correct: C**
Specific, recent, concrete loss framing with a forward projection of ongoing cost. Three wasted sprints is real and recent — it makes the status quo feel expensive rather than safe. The forward projection ("next three quarters look the same") extends the loss into a trajectory.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Vague gain framing. Sounds good but creates no urgency.
- B: Benchmark gain framing — sounds compelling but VPs have learned to discount "companies that do X see Y% improvement" claims.
- D: Social proof — the weakest form of argument for a budget conversation.

**Q12 | Framework: Black Swans**

You've been trying to launch a partnership with another team for two months. Their lead seems enthusiastic every time you meet but always has "one more thing" to finalize before they can commit. What's most likely happening and what do you do?

- A) They're a detail-oriented Analyst — give them more documentation and time
- B) There's a hidden constraint — competing priority, budget they don't have, or an approval they can't get. Ask in person: "How does this partnership affect your team's current priorities?"
- C) They're not actually interested — send a "have you given up on this?" message to force clarity
- D) The partnership terms aren't working — ask for a full renegotiation conversation

**✓ Correct: B**
"One more thing" for two months is the Black Swan signature of a hidden constraint. An Analyst with a genuine concern would have raised it by now. The calibrated "how" question in a face-to-face conversation creates the opening for them to reveal what they can't say directly.

**Why the traps fail:**
- A: Analysts express their concerns clearly, just slowly and with data. The "one more thing" pattern is evasion, not thoroughness.
- C: Would surface disinterest but gives them an easy exit before you understand the real blocker.
- D: Assumes a contract issue without evidence. The pattern suggests a constraint, not a terms problem.

**Q13 | Framework: Implementation "How" Questions**

Your CEO has just verbally approved a major product pivot in a 1:1. This is a significant strategic shift affecting multiple teams. What do you ask before leaving?

- A) "Wonderful — I'll put together an announcement and implementation plan."
- B) "How do we make sure the board is aligned with this direction before we announce it internally?"
- C) "Who else needs to know about this decision?"
- D) "What's your timeline expectation for the first visible milestone?"

**✓ Correct: B**
Forces the CEO to think about the most significant hidden stakeholder — the board — and her own role in securing alignment there. A product pivot announced internally before the board is aligned is the exact scenario that causes reversals and whiplash. Asking "how" makes her responsible for the answer.

**Why the traps fail:**
- C: Produces a list, not a plan. You do the coordination, discovering blockers only when you hit them.
- A: A verbal 1:1 approval for a major pivot is often the beginning of the alignment process, not the end of it.
- D: Timeline before alignment is the wrong sequence — you can have a perfect plan on a dead initiative.
